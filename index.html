01

How I think about systems

A user’s problem is rarely in the place it shows up. I look at the whole path a request takes — from the person, through identity and access, into the application and its data — before deciding where to act.

Illustrative model · select a layer

L1 · User

Depends on

A working device, browser and network connection, and an account that exists.

Can fail as

Outdated browser, cached session, Wi-Fi or VPN issue, wrong account, unclear instructions.

First question

Is it just this person, or everyone doing the same task?

  1. P1

    Understand the scope and impact

    Who is affected, what work is blocked, and how urgent it really is.

  2. P2

    Trace dependencies and isolate the cause

    Follow the request layer by layer instead of guessing at the symptom.

  3. P3

    Make controlled, security-conscious changes

    One change at a time, least privilege, with a way back.

  4. P4

    Verify the outcome and document the resolution

    Confirm with the affected user, then write down cause, fix and follow-up.

These are working principles, not a claim that I have administered every kind of enterprise infrastructure.

Case 01

M.D. Sky OS Logistics operations platform

Client
M.D. Sky Logistics
Live
mdskyos.com (opens in new tab) ↗
My role
Design, build, configuration, QA, deployment, client coordination
Stack
Supabase (PostgreSQL), Google sign-in, Cloudflare, GitHub

Built by eBuilt.app

The problem

A trucking and logistics company runs on coordination between people who need very different things: the owner needs the full picture, dispatchers assign and track loads, drivers need their own assignments on a phone, and customers need to see where their shipments stand. Shared spreadsheets and messages don’t give each person the right view — or keep them out of what they shouldn’t see.

Why roles are separated

Each role sees a different interface, not the same screen with buttons hidden. That keeps each person’s work simple, and it means a mistake in one view can’t expose another role’s data.

Why access boundaries matter

Hiding a menu is not security. Access has to be enforced where data is read and written, so a driver or customer who changes a URL or calls the backend directly still only gets their own records.

Why every change is tested per role

A change that works for the admin can break the driver’s view or widen what a customer can see. A feature isn’t done until it has been checked from each affected role.

Implementation, testing & reliability
Implementation
  • Separate Owner/Admin, Dispatcher, Driver and Customer experiences.
  • Authentication with Google sign-in and role-based access boundaries.
  • Logistics workflows with defined statuses and operational interfaces.
  • Cloud-hosted configuration, custom domain and DNS setup.
  • Integration work with third-party services.
Testing & reliability
  • Browser-based QA through each role’s workflow, end to end.
  • Permission checks: confirming each role can do what it should — and is refused what it shouldn’t.
  • Database-security testing of access rules, not just the interface.
  • Bug fixing followed by re-testing the affected workflows.
  • Every change tracked in Git, with rollback to a known-good version when needed.
Working with the client

Implementation, testing and deployment were coordinated directly with the business owner: confirming how her team actually works, walking through changes, and agreeing what was ready before it reached users.

Project status — kept honest

  • LiveRole-based application, authentication, core workflows, hosting and domain.
  • In progressAdditional third-party integrations (including transportation-management integration) and full operational rollout with the client’s team.

Lesson

The hardest part was not the screens; it was defining who can see and change what, then proving it holds from every side. That is the same discipline as managing access in any organization.

Case 02

My Sweet Pawtner Booking & administration system

Client
Pet-care business
Live
mysweetpawtner.com (opens in new tab) ↗
My role
Customer site, booking flow, admin portal, configuration, testing
Scope
Public website + administrative portal

Built by eBuilt.app

The problem

A small pet-care business lives on its bookings. Each request carries details that matter operationally — which service, which dates, and pickup or drop-off arrangements. If those details arrive incomplete or scattered across messages, the owner spends her time chasing information instead of caring for animals.

Workflow design

The booking form asks for what the business actually needs to deliver the service — including pickup and drop-off — so the request is usable the moment it arrives.

Reliable records & clear status

Every booking becomes one record with a clear state the owner can act on, instead of a thread to reconstruct. Status transitions should be obvious and hard to get wrong.

Interfaces people will use

Customers get a straightforward flow; the administrator gets a portal built around her day, not around the database.

Implementation, testing & integrations
Implementation
  • Customer-facing website with service information.
  • Booking flow capturing scheduling and pickup/drop-off requirements.
  • Administrative portal for managing requests.
Testing
  • Walked the booking experience end to end as a customer would.
  • Checked that submitted details arrive complete and correct on the admin side.
  • Re-tested related workflows after each change.
Integrations

Payment and automation services were planned and configured as a separate step from the core booking system, so the business can rely on bookings while integrations are verified on their own.

Project status — kept honest

  • LivePublic website, booking flow and admin portal.
  • Planned / in configurationPayment processing (Square) and automated processes — not presented here as fully operational.

Lesson

For a small business, reliability means fewer things to remember. Good workflow design removes the follow-up messages before anyone has to send them.

    Operational leadership

    Before I built systems, I ran a team that depended on them. At Prime Healthcare I progressed from agent to administrative and sales manager, supporting a team of up to 50 people.

    AgentAdministrative & sales managerTeam of up to 50

    Onboarded and trained employeesMany “technical” tickets are really training gaps. Good onboarding reduces repeat issues.
    Created procedures and training materialsDocumentation is what lets support scale beyond one person’s memory.

    When a system goes down for a team of fifty, the cost is immediate: people can’t serve customers. That experience shapes how I prioritize, communicate and verify when something breaks.

    07

    How I approach reliability

    1. R1

      Access should match responsibilities.

    2. R2

      Changes should be traceable.

    3. R3

      Recovery procedures should be understood and verified.

    4. R4

      Testing should cover affected workflows and permissions.

    5. R5

      Technical solutions should be usable by the people who depend on them.

    In my own development and deployment work I rely on Git history and rollback, saved copies, and restoring previous versions of code, files or configuration. That is application-level recovery practice — not enterprise backup infrastructure administration, which I would expect to learn within a larger team’s standards.
